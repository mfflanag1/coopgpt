                  Sharp subsidies for protecting core maxima
                      in three-sided assignment markets

                                                 Abstract
         Consider three equally sized finite sectors, nonnegative pair values, and productive teams
     containing exactly one player from each sector. A team’s value is the sum of its three pair values;
     coalition worth is maximum integral matching worth. Assume the original core is nonempty and
     grand worth is T > 0. For each player let Hi be their maximum payoff over that entire original
     core. We determine the worst-case external subsidy needed to cover every original coalition while
     paying each player at least Hi /3. Every finite market requires strictly less than T /3, and the
     supremum of the subsidy-to-worth ratio is exactly 1/3. A direct fractional-packing argument
     combines six core-maximum comparisons with residual budgets at separately attaining core
     allocations. For an integral partial matching leaving k ≥ 1 players unmatched in each sector, the
     associated subsidy obstruction is at most 2(k − 1)T /[3(2k − 1)]. This bound is sharp for every
     k ≥ 2. We give complete proofs, a family with pair values in {0, 1, 2}, and reproducible exact
     checks. A compact supplement records exact nonnegative slack identities and the verification
     scope. The original core is fixed before subsidy, and the result is static: it makes no claim about
     reporting incentives across profiles.

Keywords: assignment game; core; individual payoff maximum; subsidy; cost of stability; fractional
matching.


1    Introduction
A stable allocation need not simultaneously give each player a substantial fraction of what that
player could obtain at another stable allocation. An external budget permits a different question:
how much must be added to the grand coalition’s worth to protect individual benchmarks while
retaining all original coalition constraints?
    We study the benchmark Hi /3, where Hi is player i’s maximum over the entire original core of
a three-sided assignment market. The reference core is fixed before intervention. In particular, the
benchmark is not recomputed after adding the subsidy. The restriction to nonnegative pair-additive
team values is central; an arbitrary three-dimensional value array is not assumed to satisfy our
conclusion.
    Our main result gives the sharp worst-case subsidy ratio 1/3. It is a supremum over finite
markets of arbitrary size, not a maximum at a finite size. The upper proof applies directly to
fractional partial matchings in the subsidy dual. It does not require a reduction to sparse markets,
replication of players, or an acyclic support. A second result determines a sharp upper bound for the
obstruction supplied by an integral partial matching, indexed by its number of unmatched players
per sector.

Related work and contribution. The assignment-game connection between core allocations
and linear-programming duality is classical (Shapley and Shubik, 1971). Protection relative to
individual core maxima is also an established question. Núñez and Rafels (2004, Bargained stable
allocations in assignment markets, Barcelona Economics Working Paper 153, Definition 6; inspected
working version) study the bilateral half-optimal core: stable allocations paying every agent at
least half their maximum core payoff. Thus neither the benchmark based on core maxima nor
simultaneous fractional protection is introduced here.
     External payments to stabilize a game are studied through the cost of stability (Bachrach et al.,
2009, arXiv:0907.4385; published successor (Bachrach et al., 2018)). Meir, Rosenschein and Malizia
(Meir et al., 2011, Section 2.2) also define the cost of extending a given efficient payoff vector to a
stable one; the same definition appears in Bachrach et al. (Bachrach et al., 2018, Section 5). Their
input vector has total T ; our floor vector H/3 may have smaller total. Section 7 records the exact
comparison. Bejan and Gómez (Bejan and Gómez, 2009) define minimal grand-coalition expansions
and the extended core. The published definitions in Section 2, pp. 5–6, minimize the required
expansion over efficient payoff vectors; the extended core coincides with the core for balanced games.
These passages were inspected in the author-uploaded published-layout text and agree with the
previously read August 1, 2008 author version. Here that ordinary minimum is zero. Our additional
restriction is the simultaneous floor H/3, which need not itself be efficient or stable. Priority-game
transformations also impose individual protections. In the final version of Núñez Lugilde et al.
(2024), Definitions 2 and 4 use a prior core allocation; footnote 3 extends the formulas to imputations.
Lemmas 2 and 8 identify restrictions of the original core while retaining grand-coalition worth.
These statements do not supply the sharp subsidy constant for our possibly incompatible floors.
The subsidy framework and the operation of imposing individual claims are established antecedents.
     Atay and Núñez (Atay and Núñez, 2019, p. 273) describe a model in Quint (Quint, 1991) in which
full teams have pair-additive values and smaller coalitions have zero worth. Their own graph model
permits productive partial-sector teams and therefore has different coalition constraints. Results on
core extrema in supermodular multisided markets with local monotonicity (Martínez-de-Albéniz et
al., 2026) impose additional hypotheses absent here.
     The contribution proposed for review is the aggregate compatibility inequality and the resulting
sharp subsidy bound for the stated class. The extremal family was obtained earlier in this
project’s subsidy work; the matching upper bound and its integral refinement complete its extremal
interpretation. This manuscript gives a self-contained proof rather than claiming a new general
subsidy framework or a new assignment model. A companion manuscript, Acyclicity and simultaneous

6    The sharpness family
For an integer ℓ ≥ 1, take n = 2ℓ + 1 and labels 0, . . . , 2ℓ. Initialize A, B, C to the identity matrix.
Define special teams

                        e0 = (0, ℓ, 2ℓ),      es = (s, ℓ + s, s − 1)    (1 ≤ s ≤ ℓ − 1).                      (22)

For every e = (j1 , j2 , j3 ) in this list set Aj1 j2 = 2, Bj1 j3 = 1, and Cj2 j3 = 2. All labels within a
special team are distinct, so these entries are off-diagonal. The special teams are player-disjoint:
their sectorwise label sets are {0, . . . , ℓ − 1}, {ℓ, . . . , 2ℓ − 1}, and {2ℓ, 0, . . . , ℓ − 2}. In particular,
special entries do not conflict or overwrite diagonal entries.

Proposition 6.1 (Exact values). The market in (22) has a nonempty original core and

                           T = 3(2ℓ + 1),         Hi = 3 (i ∈ N ),        s(M ) = 2ℓ.                         (23)

Proof. Consider the bipartite incidence graph with one vertex for each label and one vertex for each
special team, joined by label membership. Consecutive special teams share one label; nonconsecutive
special teams share none, and each has a private second-sector label. Their label sets together
contain every label 0, . . . , 2ℓ. Thus the graph is connected and has (2ℓ + 1) + ℓ vertices and 3ℓ edges,
so it is a tree. This also covers ℓ = 1.
    Fix a label r and a sector a0 ∈ {1, 2, 3}. Root this incidence tree at r, assign r color a0 , and
traverse outward. When a special team is reached, its parent label is already colored; assign each
of its two other labels the sector in which that label occurs in the team. No assignments conflict

because the incidence graph is a tree. Each special team therefore has at least two labels colored by
their designated sectors. With c(j) denoting the resulting label color, set

                                     xa,j = 1{a=a0 } + 21{c(j)=a} .                                (24)

This vector is nonnegative, has total 3n, pays each diagonal team exactly 3, and satisfies xa0 ,r = 3.
    We check every full-team inequality. Each team receives a baseline payoff of 1 from the first
term in (24). If it contains two positive off-diagonal pair entries, the shared player lies in the same
special team for both entries, because special teams are player-disjoint. The team must therefore be
that special team. It has value 5 and receives at least 1 + 2 + 2 = 5. If two positive pair entries are
diagonal, all three labels coincide, and the team’s value and payoff are both 3.
    Every remaining team has at most one positive off-diagonal entry and at most one diagonal entry.
Any off-diagonal entry belongs to a special team. At least one of its endpoints has its designated
color, since at least two of the three labels in that special team do. Such a team receives at least 3.
Its value is at most 3 if the off-diagonal entry is 2, and at most 2 if that entry is 1. A nondiagonal
team with no positive off-diagonal entries has value at most 1, covered by the baseline. These cases
exhaust all teams.
    The diagonal matching has value 3n and x covers every matching with total 3n, so T = 3n and
x ∈ C. Diagonal tightness bounds every original-core coordinate by 3. As r, a0 were arbitrary, (24)
proves Hi = 3 for each player.
    Every protected allocation pays at least 1 to each player. The ℓ special teams form a partial
matching of value 5ℓ, and its 3n − 3ℓ unmatched players require at least 3n − 3ℓ. Hence the total
payment is at least 3n + 2ℓ, proving s(M ) ≥ 2ℓ. Conversely start with payoff 1 to every player and
add 2 to the first-sector player of each special team. A special team receives 5; every other team
has value at most 3 by the preceding classification and receives at least 3. This allocation covers all
coalitions, meets every floor, and has total 3n + 2ℓ. Thus s(M ) = 2ℓ.

   The ratios 2ℓ/[3(2ℓ + 1)] tend to 1/3, proving the remaining assertion of Theorem 2.2. For
the special partial matching, k = ℓ + 1 and J(M, P ) = 2ℓ, which proves the equality assertion of
Theorem 5.1.
Remark 6.2 (Why the finite bound is integral). The formula in (21) cannot be applied by inserting
a fractional packing’s residual mass k. In the family with ℓ = 1, let D be the diagonal matching and
P = {e0 }. The packing λ = 12 1D + 12 1P has T = 9, W = 7, and k = 1. Its six residual players have
qi = 1/2 and Hi = 3, so S = 9 and W + S/3 − T = 1. Substituting k = 1 in (21) would incorrectly
give zero. The fractional proof instead retains C2 , which need not equal β.

Let li = Hi /3. From diagonal caps, i li ≤ T . Following Meir et al. (Meir et al., 2011, Section 2.2)

stable payoff vector above an efficient preimputation x, with i xi = T . The following identity is a

                                   s(M ) =      min         CoS(x, G).                             (25)
                                             x≥l, x(N )=T

Indeed, if y ≥ l is stable with total at least T , there is a vector x between l and y with total T :
distribute T − l(N ) of the available nonnegative coordinate increments y − l. Conversely, a stable
extension of any such x lies above l. In particular the intervention fits an existing subsidy framework.

Its sharp quantitative behavior here comes from the structure of the assignment market and its
original maxima.
    Our universal statement allows arbitrary nonnegative real pair values, dense supports, repeated
diagonal labels within a team, and zero diagonal values. It does not assume that all maxima on
one sector are jointly attainable. Nonempty original core is an explicit hypothesis, not a claim
for all pair-additive three-sided markets. Pair-additivity and nonnegativity enter through the
incident-budget identity (9) and the completion argument in Lemma 3.1. Equal sector sizes give the
perfect diagonal matching and common residual mass. No extension to signed pair values, arbitrary
triple arrays, or a different productive-coalition convention is asserted.
    The finite integral bound does not determine the largest full subsidy at each fixed n, because
its maximizing dual may be fractional. A constructive allocation formula avoiding general linear
programming, and the sharp tradeoff for floors αHi at other values of α, are separate questions.
They are not required for the sharp constant proved here.

Verification and reproducibility. The proofs above are conventional all-parameter arguments.
The accompanying standard-library Python package verifies the coefficient identities in (16) and the
two slack identities in Online Resource 1, including all 27 ordered label triples over three symbols in
the sixfold count (covering every coincidence type). For ℓ = 1, . . . , 12, it reconstructs each separate
ideal-attaining core witness and a matching optimal subsidy allocation from the raw pair matrices.
That check covers 3,603,804 full-team inequalities and rejects 12 deliberately damaged allocations.
Additional rational packing checks include the fractional counterexample in Remark 6.2. These finite
checks corroborate, but do not establish, the universal theorem; no proof-assistant formalization is
