Abstract
Owners of complementary productive resources may gain by withholding capacity when a
sharing rule selects efficient core allocations. We ask how much external subsidy suffices to retain
every coalition’s production guarantee while making each fixed owner’s payment nondecreasing
in its reported resource bundle. For any technology represented by the minimum of K fixed
nonnegative linear forms, we construct a deterministic rule satisfying both requirements at all
nonnegative real report profiles. Its total payment is at most (1 + o(1))T log K/ log log K, where
T is grand-coalition production. A matching lower bound holds for the worst case with at most
K structural cuts even on simple directed acyclic flow networks with distinct strategic edge
owners. Thus the leading constant is one. The upper combines capped supporting prices selected
without the recipient’s report with endpoint marginal payments. The lower transports payments
across unilateral report changes and explicitly accounts for auxiliary owners. The incentive model
permits binding componentwise underreports and payment-only utility. The bound is uniform in
owner count and resource dimension, but K may be exponential in a compact description. Exact
finite evaluation exists; polynomial preprocessing is not asserted. The result isolates the cost of
linking coalition-covering allocations across reports in games whose individual profiles already
admit efficient core allocations. Forpevery finite monotone

Introduction

Resource owners can jointly create value that none can produce alone. A sharing rule must then
address two distinct problems: retaining the coalition and eliciting the resources available to it. At
a given capacity profile, competitive supporting prices can allocate the entire production value while
meeting every coalition’s outside option. The difficulty is choosing these allocations consistently
across profiles. An owner who receives less after revealing additional resources has an incentive to
withhold them.
We measure the external budget needed to reconcile these requirements. Owners, resource types
and technology are fixed. Each owner reports a nonnegative bundle and can commit no more than
1

its true resources. Its utility is its payment; resource use has no private cost. A single deterministic
rule must work at every nonnegative real profile, including zeros and ties. Payments are nonnegative,
cover every coalition’s production value, and are nondecreasing in the reporting owner’s entire
bundle with the other reports held fixed. These assumptions describe capacity disclosure by fixed
partners; they do not model hidden opportunity costs or the choice of who joins.
The production function is a minimum of K fixed nonnegative linear forms. This includes finite
nonnegative linear production programs and single-commodity flow networks. A form can represent
supporting resource prices; in a network it is a structural cut. Grand production is denoted by T .
If total payment is at most BT , the external subsidy is at most (B − 1)T . Coverage includes the
grand coalition, so total payments cannot be below T at positive-value profiles.

The upper bound selects supporting prices after randomly deleting whole owners. A recipient’s
excluded price does not depend on its own report, and its contribution is capped at grand production.
Endpoint marginal payments repair coalition coverage. Averaging over a short interval of deletion
probabilities controls the additional payment. Randomness defines a finite expectation: the
implemented rule is deterministic. The proof uses a union bound over the fixed forms, which
explains the technology-complexity parameter.

1.2

Related work and contribution boundary

Dual-price core allocations are classical in linear production games (Owen, 1975; Samet and Zemel,
1984). The present contribution concerns selecting payments across reports under a uniform budget
bound. Fixed-game cost of stability (Bachrach et al., 2018) studies subsidy for coalitional stability;
here efficient cores already exist profile by profile.
2

The closest strategic comparison is Huang, Mei, Yang and Cao’s Mechanism Design in Max-Flows
(2025, inspected arXiv:2502.08248v1, Sections 2 and 4). It uses binding capacity underreports and
payment utility, establishes an obstruction to truthful efficient core selection, and studies efficient
truthful mechanisms without universal core membership, including its Shapley rule (Proposition
1). We retain every coalition inequality and permit subsidy. We do not claim their split/merge or
other-owner monotonicity guarantees.
Immorlica et al. (2008) impose cross-monotonicity when other owners join. Brânzei et al. (2007)
define type-monotonic schemes on participating subgames of a unit-resource market (Definition 1
and Theorems 5–6 of the final 2007 text). Those comparisons change the population; ours changes
one fixed owner’s reported bundle. No equivalence via strategic cloning is used.
Amigo et al. (2016) do use own-resource monotonicity for a fixed alliance. Their inspected
author version defines a strictly concave network-utility model and studies projection onto the full
efficient core. Theorem 1 of that author version proves own-resource monotonicity of the Shapley
value, while noting that this value can fail core membership. Its projection monotonicity evidence is
computational, not a universal proof. Online Resource 1 supplies a strictly concave counterexample
to a universal extension of that evidence, with a version-specific comparison. This auxiliary example
is not an instance of our homogeneous main theorem.
Weighted multi-glove games and their total balancedness are established (Moretti and Norde,
2021). Their sharper special-case analysis, the connection to the continuous Banzhaf functional,
and additional mechanism comparisons appear in Online Resource 1. Supporting prices, marginal
contributions, concentration bounds and weighted counting are established tools. The proposed
contribution is their combination into the joint all-profile coverage, incentive and sharp budget
guarantee, with a matching lower bound under fixed ownership. The bounded source audit is not a
certification of historical priority.

source–sink flow using only its owned edges. Structural cuts are inclusion-minimal edge sets

the best all-profile eligible total-payment factor on that graph. The analogous quantity for finite
nonnegative min-linear technologies with at most K forms is Bprod (K); owner counts and dimensions
are unrestricted finite integers.

Proposition 4.2 (A subdivision that destroys the lower bound). Fully subdividing every edge of
any finite directed network into two edges owned separately produces a network with an efficient
eligible rule.
Proof. For pair reports (ae , be ) set de = min(ae , be ). Choose the original network’s fixed-priority
minimum-cut core allocation Q(d), which pays de on the selected cut and zero elsewhere. Pay Qe (d)
to the higher reporting member of pair e, split equally at equality, and pay the lower member zero.
Holding the mate’s report fixed, the payment is zero below it, half the fixed Qe at equality and the
fixed Qe above it: in the last two cases all virtual capacities are fixed. Thus it is own-monotone,
even if Q is not. For a coalition S, let S ∗ contain the original edges whose two members both lie in
S. Its value is exactly vd (S ∗ ), and p(S) ≥ Q(S ∗ ) ≥ vd (S ∗ ). Total payment is T . This shows why
the naive subdivision reduction fails and motivates the explicit auxiliary-budget accounting in the
shared-fan construction.

Implications, limitations and further questions

The result identifies technology complexity as one sharp measure of the budget required to combine
truthful disclosure with coalition coverage. For every fixed finite representation, the upper holds
simultaneously over all profiles and all owner bundles. The lower uses an increasing family of
fixed technologies; it does not change the graph when an owner changes its report. Its asymptotic
statement concerns a worst case with at most K cuts, not an exact-count construction for each
integer K.
Two questions p
remain distinct from
the sharp K theorem. First, Theorem 3.1 bounds the

over logarithmic in n and d. The supplement’s matching square-root barriers apply to specified
mask/support architectures; they do not close these unrestricted gaps. A better general rule
must escape those restrictions. Second, rational finite technologies permit exact evaluation by
finite enumeration, and the supplement develops finite sampling with all-profile certification. The

computational obstruction would be a separate contribution.
The main theorem deliberately isolates capacity disclosure. Private operating costs, endogenous
participation, and coordinated reports require additional incentive constraints. A resource-sharing
application should justify the payment-only assumption and its feasible deviations before interpreting
the bound as a policy prescription.
