                                        Abstract
            Each agent in our model occupies a position in a hierarchy (a di-
        rected tree) and generates returns through collaborating with his su-
        periors. Thus his superiors can also claim their ownership rights over
        his returns. Our main axiom for the allocation of these returns is the
        standard monotonicity with regard to the collective ownership. We es-
        tablish axiomatic characterizations of monotonic allocation rules. They
        are represented by hierarchical transfers of the returns at each position
        to the superiors. When the rate of transfer is symmetric, the rules co-
        incide with the geometric rules (ownership rates constitute a geometric
        sequence, moving up the hierarchy). Other fair allocation rules are also
        available, when the rate of transfer is asymmetric; a focal example is
        the hierarchical equal sharing rule, which is shown to be the unique
        one with an equal treatment axiom.
        JEL numbers: D63
        Keywords: revenue sharing, hierarchy, allocation rule, coalition mono-
        tonicity, reallocation-proofness, fair allocation, geometric rules, hierar-
        chical equal sharing

characterize useful families of rules that promote fairness as well as other
standard principles in the fair allocation literature.
    As in the model of Hougaard et al. (2017) and Harless (2020), each agent
occupies a position in a hierarchy and generates returns through collaborat-
ing with all those in his superior positions; thus, these superiors can also
claim their ownership rights over his returns. Therefore, in this model, a
coalition can have the collective ownership of its returns (the sum of all of
its members’ returns) if, and only if, it includes all of its superior members.
The final share of such a coalition should increase when its ownership rights,
that is, its returns increase, according to our basic principle of fairness, called
coalition monotonicity.2

    The UN Watercourses Convention (UN, 2014) states
      Watercourse states shall, in utilizing an international watercourse
      in their territories, take all appropriate measures to prevent the
      causing of significant harm to other watercourse States. [UN, 2014;
      Article 7, Clause 1]
The article establishes the obligation of states to exercise due diligence when
utilizing a watercourse. It acknowledges that a state has the right to not
be subjected to careless, negligent, or inequitable harm by its neighbors. In
the case of a polluted river (Ni and Wang, 2007), the harm occurs in the
form of river pollution, where pollutants from upstream states cause harm to
downstream states. Accordingly, the convention acknowledges transbound-
ary responsibility for polluted waters, from upstream to downstream. In
this context, coalition monotonicity acknowledges the transboundary re-
sponsibility of upstream states for harming downstream states. As their
responsibilities increase, upstream states should bear a greater cost.
     In the river sharing problem (Ambec and Sprumont, 2002; Martinez
and Moreno-Ternero, 2025), downstream states suffer harm when upstream
states monopolize water resources. The Convention, therefore, acknowledges
the transboundary water rights of downstream states over the upstream
water resources. Coalition monotonicity, in this case, is also a basic principle
that respects the transboundary water rights of downstream states.
     In the revenue sharing literature, unfair trading practices in supply
chains are prevalent, and an important policy issue is how to rectify in-
equitable distribution of returns (Sexton, 2017). Fair supply chain manage-
ment is the standard policy objective in EU countries since it improves long-
term economic performance and sustainability of supply chains (Touboulic et
al., 2014). Imbalanced supply chain relationships often arise due to unequal
bargaining power in each pair of a principal contractor and a subcontractor
(Glöckner, 2017). Our investigation offers a formal, quantitative approach
for fair supply chain management by identifying fair trading practices with
our fair allocation (redistribution) rules that can be used to establish con-
crete policy objectives. For example, when the bargaining power of firm i is
stronger than that of neighboring firm j in the supply chain, i takes more
returns than its “fair” share. Then, in the process of fair redistribution, j
has claims over i’s share. Likewise, a hierarchy of these claims arises from

unequal pairwise bargaining power in the supply chain. A fair allocation
rule in our model may be applied to rectify unfair trading practices and the
resulting distribution of initial returns.
    We establish a representation theorem (Theorem 1) for well-behaved
rules satisfying coalition monotonicity. These rules are represented by “up-
ward transfers” of returns at each position in the hierarchy to immediate
superiors; hence we call them hierarchical transfer rules. A focal example is
the hierarchical equal sharing rule, which is shown to be the unique hierar-
chical transfer rule satisfying an equal treatment property (Theorem 2).4
    When the rate of transfer is symmetric (or constant), hierarchical trans-
fer rules are called the geometric rules (introduced by Hougaard et al., 2017)
since the ownership rights of superiors over an inferior’s returns form a “ge-
ometric” sequence. A new characterization of geometric rules is obtained as
a corollary (Corollary 1) to the representation theorem.
    Hougaard et al. (2017) examine which allocation rules are non-manipulable
when a highest rank agent can introduce dummy agents and split his re-
turns between himself and the dummies. They show that geometric rules
are the only well-behaved rules that are non-manipulable by highest rank
splitting (or merging). In our model, the set of agents is fixed, so splitting
or merging is not admissible. Nevertheless, agents may form a coalition and
reallocate their returns and by doing so, they can get more just as they
could by splitting or merging. Moulin (1985) introduces non-manipulability
by reallocation, which is called reallocation-proofness by Ju et al. (2007)
and Ju (2013). In our model, such reallocation is admissible only for a
coalition inclusive of all superiors, which makes reallocation-proofness much
weaker than that in the earlier studies. Our results also hold when coali-
tion monotonicity is replaced with reallocation-proofness. Nevertheless, the
monotonicity requirement beyond reallocation-proofness plays an essential
role, as demonstrated by our results with strong coalition monotonicity.
    Hierarchical transfer rules are asymmetric generalizations of geometric
rules in Hougaard et al. (2017), obtained by relaxing two symmetry condi-
tions of geometric rules. First, the rate of transfer to the superiors may vary
across different positions in the hierarchy. Second, when there are multiple
immediate superiors, the rates of transfers to them may be unequal.

    The next example is the problem of profit sharing in supply chains.

Example 2 (Profit sharing in a supply chain). Let N be the set of firms
in a supply chain, which is an undirected tree. Unfair business practices
due to imbalanced bargaining power result in an inequitable distribution
of profits r (Glöckner, 2017). Assume that for any pair of directly linked
firms, say i and j, either i’s bargaining power is stronger than j’s or j’s is
stronger than i’s. In the former case, i’s profit ri is above i’s “fair share”
and so the weaker party j has legitimate claims over i’s profit. Thus the
imbalanced distribution of powers gives rise to the hierarchy of firms’ claims
or rights, making the weaker party (firm j) an immediate superior to the
stronger party (firm i) in the hierarchy. The problem in this context is
how to redistribute the initial distribution of profits r while respecting the
ownership rights of the unfairly treated (superiors in the hierarchy).

    “Sharing a river” is another example studied by Ambec and Sprumont (2002)
and Martı́nez and Moreno-Ternero (2025). When all agents have a common
linear schedule of benefits from water resources as in Martı́nez and Moreno-
Ternero (2025), it is an example of our model. The two earlier investigations
focus on a linear hierarchy. Our model extends it to the general hierarchy.

Example 3 (Sharing a river). Let N be the set of n states sharing a river.

i’s downstream states over ri . Thus it gives rise to the hierarchy of states

Note that under the geometric rule with λ, for each non-top agent i ∈ N \T , if
none of his immediate superiors is a top agent, the ownership of ri distributed
to all of i’s immediate superiors equals (1 − λ)λri . Likewise, the ownership
of ri distributed to all immediate superiors of each immediate superior of
i, assuming again none of them is a top agent, is (1 − λ)2 λri , and so on.
Thus, the ownership over ri diminishes at the constant rate of (1 − λ),
according to a “geometric” sequence, moving up the hierarchy. This feature
of diminishing ownership over ri no longer applies to top agents, because all
top agents enjoy the full ownership independently of λ.
    The geometric rule with λ = 0 gives no ownership to any non-top agent
over his returns, and top agents “monopolize” all the returns of their sub-
ordinates (non-top agents). Hence, we call it the top-monopoly geometric
rule. The geometric rule with λ = 1 gives full ownership to all agents over
their returns and there is no transfer to superiors; thus, it is called the no
transfer rule.

4        Results
Our first result shows that only hierarchical transfer rules satisfy (strong)
coalition monotonicity, efficiency, highest rank returns independence, inde-
pendence of irrelevant agents, component null-consistency, and scale invari-
ance. Independence of irrelevant agents is redundant when the hierarchy
has a single top agent. Efficiency is redundant when two or more top agents
exist and strong coalition monotonicity is assumed.
Theorem 1. (i) A rule satisfies coalition monotonicity, efficiency, highest
rank returns independence, independence of irrelevant agents, component
null-consistency, and scale invariance if and only if it is a hierarchical trans-
fer rule. When there is a unique top agent, this equivalence holds without
independence of irrelevant agents.
(ii) When two or more top agents exist in the hierarchy, a rule satisfies
strong coalition monotonicity, highest rank returns independence, indepen-
dence of irrelevant agents, component null-consistency, and scale invariance
if and only if it is a hierarchical transfer rule.
    The proof is provided in the appendix. The two sets of axioms in the
theorem are independent.18
    We now consider the standard equal treatment property: any two agents
with equal “deserts” should be treated equally. Its definition depends on how
different positions in a hierarchy affect their deserts.
    Our first definition concerns the social norm, position neutrality that all
contributors (superiors) to the returns at each position have equal deserts
independently of their positions. In the example of an international conven-
tion of watercourses, it requires that watercourse states have equal deserts
for their upstream waters independent of their position. Consider a returns
profile where only subordinates of agent i ∈ N have non-zero returns. Po-
sition neutrality means in such a problem that agent i and all i’s superiors
(in sp(i)) have equal deserts for i’s returns ri and the returns of i’s subor-
dinates. Note that i’s superiors have no other contribution in the problem,
since all the other returns are zero. Hence they all have equal deserts (for
the total returns). The equal treatment property requires, in this case, that
all these agents (i and i’s superiors) with equal deserts should be treated
equally.

variability in the model.

1. An important implication of coalition monotonicity is that the total payoff
of any coalition with the collective ownership of its returns is independent
of how the returns are distributed among its members, which implies the
following non-manipulability by reallocation.20

elaborated on by Moulin (1985).
