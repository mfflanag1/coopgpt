Foundations: what questions are we asking?
The common question is how independent people or organizations can share a scarce resource or jointly create value—and still have reasons to accept the outcome. Different parts of this program give different meanings to “fair,” “stable,” and “truthful.”
Game theory: choices change other people’s outcomes
A game specifies players, their available actions, information, and payoffs. A Nash equilibrium is an action profile at which no player can improve its payoff by changing only its own action. That is a statement about unilateral incentives under a specified game. It need not maximize total output or produce a fair division. A different institution changes the available actions and can change the equilibrium.
Mechanism design asks how to choose that institution: which reports are accepted, which allocation is implemented, and which payments are made. For example, an infrastructure consortium might allocate scarce power and pay owners for committing hardware. Whether an owner can misstate a valuation, withhold real capacity, split into false identities, or withdraw and produce elsewhere matters to the theorem. “Truthful” is meaningful only after those choices are defined.
Roughgarden’s mechanism-design lecture
provides a standard entry point.
Algorithmic game theory: the institution must also be computable
Algorithmic game theory combines incentive questions with algorithms, optimization, and computational complexity. It asks whether an equilibrium or fair allocation exists, whether it can be found efficiently, what information is required, and what approximation is possible. A mathematically defined payment rule can exist even when computing it requires exponentially many coalitions or cuts. A finite exact solver can certify a small instance without providing a scalable algorithm.
This portfolio connects most directly to algorithmic mechanism design, cooperative optimization, fair division, and exact certification. It is not primarily a study of learning dynamics or the price of anarchy. The relevant computer-science questions include efficient separation oracles, max-flow implementations, linear-programming certificates, and verification over continuous report domains.

explains the broader field;

Exact finite duals and grid completeness.
For one fixed finite institution, the supremum of nested dyadic-grid optima equals the all-real optimum. With rational technology and rational endowments, strict lower-factor refutation can therefore be searched through finite exact certificates. This is a compactness statement, not a finite upper certificate or an efficient approximation algorithm.
Regularization and homogenization.
Arbitrary, Borel, and facewise-smooth rules have the same funding infimum. A nonconstructive invariant-mean construction gives a homogeneous replacement without increasing the allowed factor. It may change the original rule’s values and need not be computable or continuous across zero faces. Positive endowment magnitudes do not change the optimum when the eligible coordinates and full underreport boxes remain fixed. These statements depend on funding at each

Why pointwise search is insufficient.
At every productive profile, a frozen optimal-price formula is a valid local mechanism at every factor above one on a sufficiently small neighborhood. Locally valid formulas need not agree or satisfy cross-profile comparisons. An isolated feasible Jacobian is not a globally defined payment rule.

2. Counting: exact contribution and certification
There exists a printed strictly positive integer additive valuation matrix in EACH of (n,m)=(4,7),(5,8),(6,9),(4,8),(4,9),(4,10) with exactly 4,5,6,4,4,4 complete labeled EFX allocations respectively. Each witness gives an upper bound n on the minimum possible count in its parameter cell. None proves the unrestricted minimum is n. This is six examples of one extremal phenomenon, not six general theorems.
For arbitrary finitely many additive agents with nonnegative values, let B be an EFX_0 allocation of all but one good f. Adding f to a chosen host h preserves EFX_0 exactly when, for every k != h,
v_k(B_k) >= v_k(B_h)+v_k(f)-min_{g in B_h union {f}} v_k(g).
The enlarged host is nonempty even if B_h was empty. This parking criterion follows directly from the definition; it certifies the displayed completions but cannot exclude other allocations. Exhaustive enumeration supplies that exclusion.
Separately, for EVERY matrix V in {1,2,3,4}^{4x7}, there are at least four complete labeled EFX_0 allocations, conditional on the documented mathematical-to-CNF translation and checker trust base. The retained independent literal-slot CNF uses 1,240 distinct pooled allocations, 282,029 variables and 600,624 clauses. Protected-slot soundness says that a matrix with at most three qualifying allocations could place them in three protected slots and satisfy every finite-pool constraint. DoubleLex requires its own orbit-representative argument. A checked UNSAT proof therefore excludes that finite class; this is not enumeration of all 4^28 matrices.

P1
Structural classification / assignment markets
Acyclicity and simultaneous protection of individual core maxima
The pattern of interaction between sectors determines whether a stable allocation can protect everyone relative to their own best stable payoff. Trees permit a uniform guarantee. Cycles can defeat even the best choice of stable allocation as markets grow.
Contribution.
A tree–cycle classification that applies both to the leximin rule and to the best simultaneous protection achievable anywhere in the entire core.
The mathematics and its scope
c(G) = R(G) = 1/K for trees;
c(G) = R(G) = 0 for cyclic connected graphs.
G is a fixed connected allowed interaction graph on K ≥ 2 sectors. Sector sizes are equal; pair matrices are nonnegative real matrices, with zero matrices allowed. Only complete teams produce; coalition values come from integral matchings. Require positive total worth and a nonempty entire core. With Hᵢ the individual core maximum, c(G) is the worst leximin ideal fraction and R(G) the worst best-achievable simultaneous ideal fraction. The triangle family has exact best fraction 1/(2q+3), and selective subdivision transfers the obstruction to general cyclic graphs.
Boundary of the claim.
Zero is an infimum over growing finite markets. Every single finite market still has positive best simultaneous protection. Integral matching does not mean integer-valued input weights. This theorem does not cover arbitrary productive partial teams.
Review and priority.
The October 14 source and 11-page PDF match the release hashes. Scoped internal audits are recorded. Bilateral half-ideal protection, matching duality and vanishing guarantees in broader classes have antecedents; the strict-model classification is the proposed contribution. P2
Fairness selectors / exact games
Sharp asymptotic separation of opposing egalitarian core selections
Two rules that both favor equality can distribute almost all the same surplus to different people. This paper determines how quickly their largest possible disagreement approaches that extreme as the population grows.
Contribution.
A sharp first-order quantitative comparison between opposing fairness selectors, combining a universal convex overlap inequality with explicit whole-core constructions.
The mathematics and its scope
sₙ = sup ‖Lmin(v) − Lmax(v)‖₁ / T
2 − sₙ = 2/n + O(log n / n²),
so n(2 − sₙ) → 2.
The supremum is over singleton-normalized exact transferable-utility games with n players and T = v(N) > 0. The upper argument applies to nonnegative compact convex constant-total sets; whole-core Fibonacci segments realize the matching asymptotic lower. Lmax here minimizes descending sorted payoffs. Half the normalized L1 distance is the fraction of surplus that changes recipients.
Boundary of the claim.
This is not an exact finite-n formula, a unique-extremizer theorem or an aggregate welfare-loss claim. An explicit subset of the core would not suffice: the entire-core characterization is essential.

One owner controls local energy, one shared grid capacity, and separate owners control hardware pods. Requiring the local-energy owner to receive at least θ of its grand-coalition marginal contribution gives sharp uniform total factor 1 + θ/4 for 0 ≤ θ ≤ 1. A short mathematical glossary
Coalition value
v(S) is what a group S can produce alone. T = v(N) is grand-coalition value.
Core
Allocations x with Σᵢxᵢ = T and Σᵢ∈S xᵢ ≥ v(S) for every coalition. Each group receives at least its stand-alone value.
Exact game
Every coalition’s constraint is attained at some core allocation. Different coalitions may need different allocations.
Individual ideal
Hᵢ = max{xᵢ : x is in the original core}. These individual best payoffs need not be simultaneously attainable.
Lmin and Lmax
Lmin maximizes the increasingly sorted payoff vector lexicographically. The project’s Lmax minimizes the decreasingly sorted vector lexicographically. Both favor equality; Lmax does not maximize one person’s payoff.
Funding vs subsidy
Total funding ≤ BT means extra subsidy ≤ (B − 1)T. A 4/3 funding factor means a one-third subsidy, not a 4/3 subsidy.
All-profile incentives
The same payment rule must deter withholding at every allowed report profile, with actual owners fixed. Separate core allocations at each profile do not automatically form a truthful mechanism.
n, d, K and θ
Usually n counts owners and d resource dimensions. K means sectors in graph/static results, or supporting forms/cuts in production results; these are different parameters. θ is the public contribution-share floor in the new energy contract.

Model, assumptions and explicit payment formula
Reports are binding resource commitments. Owners are fixed and may withhold their own resources coordinatewise; utility is payment only. The theorem does not establish incentives for private values, private opportunity costs, overreports, collusion or false names.

The mathematical first target and the approaches already blocked
When child components meet a new shared resource, the exact production identity is
F = A + min(H, P + M).
Here A is production without the boundary resources and M is their aggregate marginal contribution. M need not be own-report monotone: on a child path h–ℓ–u,
A = min(ℓ, u),   M = min(h, (ℓ − u)
+
).
Increasing an owner’s internal substitute u can increase A while decreasing M. Feeding M into an individually monotone primitive is insufficient. A compensated separator lemma must keep both effects in the same owner’s payment accounting.
The induction must maintain one constant at every depth. It must not reuse the already blocked virtual-vertex-core majorant, fixed component covers, or independent signed charges that ignore cap hiding. A counterexample to one invariant is a method obstruction; an unrestricted lower bound requires coalition constraints linked by actual unilateral report transitions.

Proposed specialist questions
Three contracts, different questions
Individual protection
One actual owner restores resources. Its own payment must not fall. With outside production, the increase must also cover what those resources can produce separately.
Coalition monotonicity
Members may coordinate their reports and transfer money among themselves. Their total payment must not fall when their resources are restored. We abbreviate this as TCWM.
Coalition outside production
The coalition can also pool the withheld resources and produce elsewhere. Its payment increase must cover that joint outside value. This is the strongest incentive contract displayed; additional bargaining floors are separate constraints.
The scalar-demand counterpart of TCWM is Moulin–Sprumont’s established Strong Group Monotonicity. The proposed contributions here concern quantitative funding and resource-report domains. A protected coalition member may restore zero resources while another member changes its report. Owner identities and eligibility remain fixed across reports. All-profile proofs cover real feasible withdrawals; finite computations are evidence or discovery tools, not substitutes for those proofs.

Every eligible nonnegative, report-independent weighting of whole-owner marginal contributions requires Ω_r(s^(r−1)) funding. A matching-order construction makes the optimal architecture factor Θ_r(s^(r−1)) when activities use at most r resources and coalitions up to size s receive incentive protection. This includes asymmetric and population-dependent weights. An exact 16-owner min3 certificate requires factor 5 for groups up to four.
Scope and verification
These are architecture bounds. General mechanisms on that same min3 technology have factor 2. The unrestricted arity-three interval remains Ω(s^(1/4)) to O(s²). The limiting-measure proof does not impose a finite-population support restriction. The final root66 corollary shows that report-dependent positive weights escape the barrier on min2; the owner-uniform min3 dummy-zero question remains open.

A credible AI and energy bridge
The clean candidate is several independently owned load portfolios sharing one electrical delivery point and a common reliability standard. With public Gaussian factors Lᵢ=mᵢ+⟨xᵢ,Z⟩, Z∼N(0,I), a common positive reliability quantile zα, and firm capacity Cᵢ, define tᵢ=(Cᵢ−mᵢ)/zα. Admit owner portfolios only when tᵢ≥‖xᵢ‖₂, and require both retained and removed bundles to remain in that reserve cone. Total usable pooled headroom is zα[Σtᵢ−‖Σxᵢ‖₂]. The additional headroom created by pooling is zαG=zα[Σ‖xᵢ‖₂−‖Σxᵢ‖₂].
This gives an exact conditional mapping to the norm theorem. It does not establish that AI loads fit the model, that all relevant withdrawals are cone-feasible, or that a sponsor should pay the chosen mechanism’s subsidy.
Before an empirical claim: fix actual ownership units, capacity entitlements, feasible withdrawals, curtailment allocation, reliability targets and sponsor value. Then test measured simultaneous load traces on a chronological holdout with dependence-aware uncertainty. Existing Mac measurements and request traces do not validate this model.
An efficient Euler allocation can fail withholding incentives, yet two owners have an efficient full-outside alternative. A vulnerable baseline alone does not establish that subsidy is necessary. Sale proceeds valued at the same public price cannot finance payments above that value: the sponsor needs a separately identified benefit or budget.
