    Foundations: what questions are we asking?

    The common question is how independent people or organizations can share a scarce resource or jointly create value—and still have reasons to accept the outcome. Different parts of this program give different meanings to “fair,” “stable,” and “truthful.”

    Game theory: choices change other people’s outcomes

    A game specifies players, their available actions, information, and payoffs. A Nash equilibrium is an action profile at which no player can improve its payoff by changing only its own action. That is a statement about unilateral incentives under a specified game. It need not maximize total output or produce a fair division. A different institution changes the available actions and can change the equilibrium.

    Mechanism design asks how to choose that institution: which reports are accepted, which allocation is implemented, and which payments are made. For example, an infrastructure consortium might allocate scarce power and pay owners for committing hardware. Whether an owner can misstate a valuation, withhold real capacity, split into false identities, or withdraw and produce elsewhere matters to the theorem. “Truthful” is meaningful only after those choices are defined. Roughgarden’s mechanism-design lecture provides a standard entry point.

    Algorithmic game theory: the institution must also be computable

    Algorithmic game theory combines incentive questions with algorithms, optimization, and computational complexity. It asks whether an equilibrium or fair allocation exists, whether it can be found efficiently, what information is required, and what approximation is possible. A mathematically defined payment rule can exist even when computing it requires exponentially many coalitions or cuts. A finite exact solver can certify a small instance without providing a scalable algorithm.

    This portfolio connects most directly to algorithmic mechanism design, cooperative optimization, fair division, and exact certification. It is not primarily a study of learning dynamics or the price of anarchy. The relevant computer-science questions include efficient separation oracles, max-flow implementations, linear-programming certificates, and verification over continuous report domains. Roughgarden’s introductory lecture explains the broader field; Tardos’s course map places cooperative games, cost sharing, and bandwidth sharing within it.

    Cooperative game theory: what can each group do alone?

    A transferable-utility game assigns a value v(S) to every coalition S of players. The full population is N, and T=v(N) is the value available from full cooperation. Transferable utility means the model treats this value as divisible among participants in a common payoff unit. This is an economic assumption; GPU memory, electricity, and service quality are not automatically interchangeable money.

    Core(v) = {x : ∑i∈Nxi = T and ∑i∈Sxi ≥ v(S) for every S ⊆ N}.

    The first condition is efficiency: distribute exactly the available value. The second is coalition coverage: each group receives at least its stand-alone productive value. A core allocation therefore removes a particular reason for a group to leave. It does not by itself identify a fair allocation, settle which core point to choose, or make a rule truthful as resources change. The core may be empty. Linear-production games have classical connections between supporting prices, dual optimization, and core payoffs; those ingredients are prior work, not inventions of this project. Samet and Zemel’s original core–dual paper is one directly inspected source.

    A two-owner example

    One owner supplies hardware and the other supplies power. Each alone produces zero; together they produce value 1. Every division (x,1−x), with 0≤x≤1, is in the core. Each owner’s best core payoff is 1, but both cannot receive 1 from a total of 1.

    If a contract protects a fraction α of each owner’s individual ideal, the least total funding is max{1,2α}. The excess is max{0,2α−1}. This elementary illustration explains why simultaneous protection can need subsidy; it is not a simulation of the research mechanisms.

    Fairness inside the core

    For player i, the individual ideal is Hi=max{xi:x∈Core(v)}. The maximizers for different players can be different allocations. Protecting everyone’s ideal simultaneously is consequently a geometric problem, not a matter of selecting each person’s favorite point separately.

    The project’s Lmin maximizes the increasingly sorted payoff vector lexicographically: first improve the worst payoff, then the second worst, and so on. Its Lmax minimizes the decreasingly sorted vector lexicographically: first reduce the largest payoff, then the next largest. Both pursue equality from opposite ends. Neither is a welfare-loss measure. The normalized distance ‖Lmin−Lmax‖1/T measures disagreement about who receives the same total surplus; half that distance is the fraction reassigned.

    An exact game has, for every coalition S, some core point satisfying x(S)=v(S). Different coalitions may need different points. This is a stronger property than having a nonempty core, and is distinct from “exact arithmetic” or a computer-verified certificate. A stable core in the von Neumann–Morgenstern sense is another condition about domination of allocations. It must not be substituted for ordinary core membership or for a particular procedural egalitarian rule.

    From a static division to a rule on every report

    In the production papers, actual owner i controls a vector bi of resources. The public technology F maps aggregate resources to value. Under a binding report, an owner can withhold resources it really owns. At report profile b, the induced coalition value is vb(S)=F(∑i∈Sbi). One payment rule y(b) must work throughout the entire permitted report domain. Choosing an unrelated core point for each profile does not ensure that an owner benefits from revealing more resources.

    Owners remain the same as reports change. Splitting a mathematical coordinate into several analytic terms cannot silently turn one strategic owner into several independent players. Many early proof attempts failed at precisely this interface between decomposition and ownership.

    Four numerical distinctions that prevent misreading the results

    1. Total funding versus subsidy. A guarantee ∑yi≤BT means extra funding at most (B−1)T. A 3/2 total factor is a 50% subsidy ceiling, not a 150% subsidy.
    2. Total production versus gain from cooperation. Write F(B)=S+G, with S=∑F(bi). A total-output bound κ(S+G) does not imply a constant coefficient K in S+KG. When G is small, the difference can be decisive.
    3. Sharp worst case versus a typical instance. A matching lower family proves a worst-case limit over the stated class. It does not say every market needs the same subsidy. A supremum can be approached by growing finite markets without being attained by any finite market.
    4. A mechanism-class barrier versus unrestricted impossibility. A counterexample to fixed masks, common prices, positive fixed weights, or a specific envelope estimate leaves other mechanisms available. An unrestricted lower bound must constrain every admissible rule.

    EFX: fairness when the goods themselves are indivisible

    EFX belongs to algorithmic fair division. A complete allocation gives each indivisible good to one agent. With additive values, ui(A)=∑g∈Aui(g). For strictly positive values, EFX requires ui(Ai)≥ui(Aj\{g}) for every pair i,j and every good g in j’s bundle. Envy disappears after removing any one good from the envied bundle. EF1 only asks for some good whose removal does that. With zero-valued goods, conventions about which goods are tested must be stated.

    For two people who value each of three identical goods at 1, a two-goods/one-good division is EFX: the poorer person no longer envies after any good is removed from the richer bundle. It is not envy-free before removal. Money transfers are not part of this basic allocation problem, so a cooperative-core subsidy result cannot simply be applied to it.

    The connection is methodological: existence under fairness constraints, the geometry of feasible allocations, exact counterexamples, and sound certificates. Counting how many EFX allocations an instance has is different from proving that every instance has one. The EFX chapter separates the counting paper, the historical existence certificate, and the unfinished global-admission route.

    The exact contract: what the lower bound is about

    Each institution fixes its actual owners, public technology, eligible resource coordinates, and finite endowment boxes. Production is the finite nonnegative packing value F(c)=max{π·x : Ax≤c, x≥0}, with no profitable resource-free activity. The primary theorem permits at most three input rows per activity. Different instances may have more owners and resources; within a comparison they remain identical.

    Every coordinatewise real binding underreport is legal. At every report, nonnegative payments cover every coalition: Σᵢ∈C yᵢ(b)≥F(Σᵢ∈C bᵢ). Incentive protection applies to every owner subset of size at most s, including protected members who do not change their report. TCWM says restoration cannot lower that coalition’s payment sum. Full OUT requires the increase to cover production from the restored bundle alone.

    The budget is Σᵢ yᵢ(b)≤K(s)F(Σᵢ bᵢ) at each report. It is total funding relative to reported total output, not extra subsidy or funding only from cooperation gains. There is no dummy-zero condition, prescribed mechanism, anonymity requirement, continuity assumption, or extra payment floor. Positive payments to a zero-report owner may matter when aggregate production is positive. A TCWM lower applies to full OUT; a TCWM upper need not.

    Why the square root appears: the complete proof idea

    Index resources by the nonzero vectors of 𝔽₂M+1. Every triple {a,b,a+b} is a unit-profit production activity. Choose independent vectors v,e₁,…,eM. At stage m, one owner supplies the cap v, each high owner supplies eⱼ, and low owner j supplies v+eⱼ exactly when j≤m. The supported productive activities are precisely the active branches. Each needs the cap, so total production remains exactly one.

    All owners are actually eligible to report every resource. Finite owner permutations and invertible linear transformations preserve the entire legal institution. Averaging any hypothetical rule over those symmetries preserves its constraints. At the first stage, symmetry and coverage force each of the three productive owners to receive at least one third. At stage m, the 2m active private owners have one common payment qₘ.

    Restore one actual low owner and protect it together with the 2m−2 old active private owners. The old protected reports do not change. TCWM, retaining the restorer’s nonnegative old payment, forces

    (2m−1)qₘ ≥ (2m−2)qₘ₋₁,
    QM = ∏m=2M 2m/(2m−1) = 4M / [2 binom(2M,M)].

    Thus qM ≥ QM/(3M). Terminal branch coverage gives z+2qM≥1, where z is the cap owner’s payment. Adding the remaining private payments yields K≥1+2(M−1)QM/(3M). The central-binomial asymptotic makes QM proportional to √M.

    For a fixed protected size s, later stages use only min(s−1,2m−2) old protected owners. Let h=ceil(s/2). Optimizing this particular ladder gives equal maxima at M=s and M=s+1:

    Bₛ = 1 + [2(s−1)Qh/(3h)] [(s−1)/s]s−h,   s≥2;
    B₁=1,   Bₛ/√s → √(2π)/(3√e).

    For s=6, B₆=493/243, witnessed by 13 actual fully eligible owners and 127 resource rows. This exceeds two without contradicting the factor-two TCWM theorem for a single three-input minimum technology: the projective technology is a larger class.

    Higher arity, pure ownership, and the limits of the lower-bound route

    Minimal binary circuits with r=k+1 resources replace each private pair by k private inputs. Their fixed-owner ladder satisfies [k(m−1)+1]qₘ≥k(m−1)qₘ₋₁, giving the exponent (r−2)/(r−1). The fully eligible construction keeps owner count linear in ladder length; its resources grow exponentially. A separate pure-owner realization assigns one actual owner to each resource. It preserves the lower exponent at the cost of an exponential owner count. At-most-count statements therefore retain a logarithm of resource dimension, and a logarithm of owner count in the pure realization.

    The binary unit-worth route has a proved ceiling. For linear three-uniform unit technologies with pure binary unit owners, consider the entire downward report ideal where production is at most one. Every productive profile has star structure, and an explicit payment recurrence attains Bₛ for both TCWM and full OUT. More examples inside that exact domain cannot improve the exponent. The theorem deliberately does not extend this rule to the all-real report box.

    Complete fibers over one fixed binary subspace do not evade that ceiling by cloning rows. A fiber of size R multiplies production by R, but its restoration uses R protected owner slots. In the unit-worth quotient domain, the worst factor is one when s<R, and the stated real-parameter version B(s/R) otherwise, uniformly O(√max{1,s/R}). Partial pieces of unchanged fibers are included in protected coalitions; partial-fiber reports, changing subspaces, and higher quotient worth are outside the theorem.

    Finite projective and ternary-profile LPs remain diagnostic evidence. A ternary cap-switch example forces 10/9 under singleton incentives, showing why fixed-bundle binary participation is too small a model. It does not establish a new asymptotic exponent. Activation and row-compression obstructions also have explicit symmetry and route hypotheses; they do not exclude every possible amplification.

    General construction tools, and a concrete ownership-sensitive calibration

    Exact finite duals and grid completeness. For one fixed finite institution, the supremum of nested dyadic-grid optima equals the all-real optimum. With rational technology and rational endowments, strict lower-factor refutation can therefore be searched through finite exact certificates. This is a compactness statement, not a finite upper certificate or an efficient approximation algorithm.

    Regularization and homogenization. Arbitrary, Borel, and facewise-smooth rules have the same funding infimum. A nonconstructive invariant-mean construction gives a homogeneous replacement without increasing the allowed factor. It may change the original rule’s values and need not be computable or continuous across zero faces. Positive endowment magnitudes do not change the optimum when the eligible coordinates and full underreport boxes remain fixed. These statements depend on funding at each reported aggregate, not merely a constant budget based on endowment.

    Local incentive tests. For a supplied smooth rule, coalition monotonicity can be checked by sorting the most negative Jacobian entries. Full OUT also tests assignments of each activity’s at most r inputs to actual restoring owners. For fixed arity, this is polynomial local separation at one Jacobian. Coverage, a continuum of reports, consistency of derivatives, and directed admission from zero faces remain separate obligations.

    Why pointwise search is insufficient. At every productive profile, a frozen optimal-price formula is a valid local mechanism at every factor above one on a sufficiently small neighborhood. Locally valid formulas need not agree or satisfy cross-profile comparisons. An isolated feasible Jacobian is not a globally defined payment rule.

    A finite warning for the next price-based route. On one twelve-profile, three-owner fixture, arbitrary payments have exact finite optimum 5/4. Requiring each payment to dominate some efficient production-price allocation raises the optimum to 3/2. The prices are allowed to use the entire profile, but they are uniquely forced at the six high profiles. This is a verified cost of an extra design restriction. It is not a growing-family lower, an all-real upper, or a rejection of every linear-in-s price-plus-reserve mechanism.

    Acyclicity and simultaneous protection of individual core maxima

    The pattern of interaction between sectors determines whether a stable allocation can protect everyone relative to their own best stable payoff. Trees permit a uniform guarantee. Cycles can defeat even the best choice of stable allocation as markets grow.

    Balanced transport at the egalitarian core: a complete boundary phase diagram

    At a fairness-optimal allocation, the richer players may receive strictly more than they can produce alone. This paper identifies exactly how many poorer and richer players are needed for that slack to be possible.

    A short mathematical glossary

    Coalition value
    v(S) is what a group S can produce alone. T = v(N) is grand-coalition value.
    Core
    Allocations x with Σᵢxᵢ = T and Σᵢ∈S xᵢ ≥ v(S) for every coalition. Each group receives at least its stand-alone value.
    Exact game
    Every coalition’s constraint is attained at some core allocation. Different coalitions may need different allocations.
    Individual ideal
    Hᵢ = max{xᵢ : x is in the original core}. These individual best payoffs need not be simultaneously attainable.
    Lmin and Lmax
    Lmin maximizes the increasingly sorted payoff vector lexicographically. The project’s Lmax minimizes the decreasingly sorted vector lexicographically. Both favor equality; Lmax does not maximize one person’s payoff.
    Funding vs subsidy
    Total funding ≤ BT means extra subsidy ≤ (B − 1)T. A 4/3 funding factor means a one-third subsidy, not a 4/3 subsidy.
    All-profile incentives
    The same payment rule must deter withholding at every allowed report profile, with actual owners fixed. Separate core allocations at each profile do not automatically form a truthful mechanism.
    n, d, K and θ
    Usually n counts owners and d resource dimensions. K means sectors in graph/static results, or supporting forms/cuts in production results; these are different parameters. θ is the public contribution-share floor in the new energy contract.

    A public participation floor, with an exact funding price.

    One owner A controls local energy allowances across pods. A second owner C controls shared grid capacity. Each hardware owner Hi contributes hardware at one pod. Output needs hardware and either that pod’s local energy or shared grid power.

    Public heterogeneous values: what extends, and what does not

    What the production model actually describes.

    Each branch has hardware and a local resource such as an energy allowance. Shared grid capacity can supply hardware that its local resource cannot serve. An owner may hold resources across any number of branches and roles; more than one owner may contribute to the same resource pool.

    F = min(H, P + M)H = total hardware, P = total shared grid capacity,

    This is a stylized production model. The funding guarantee is not a measured GPU throughput gain, energy saving, scheduling result or latency guarantee. Private operating costs, overreports, false names and coordinated deviations require different incentive constraints.

    The precise payment construction and why incentives are automatic

    The two-input primitive pays each owner a mixture of its short-pool contribution and its capped long-pool contribution. At short total T > 0, set di = min(longi, T), ρ = Σdi/T and q = e1−ρ/2:

    Φi = (1 − q) shorti + qdi.

    At T = 0 all primitive payments are zero. Sum the branch payments to obtain zi; apply the same primitive to Hi and pi + zi to obtain raw payment Ni. Then define

    Ei(b) = sup0 ≤ b′i ≤ bi Ni(b′i, b−i).

    Three contracts, different questions

    Individual protection

    One actual owner restores resources. Its own payment must not fall. With outside production, the increase must also cover what those resources can produce separately.

    Coalition monotonicity

    Members may coordinate their reports and transfer money among themselves. Their total payment must not fall when their resources are restored. We abbreviate this as TCWM.

    Coalition outside production

    The coalition can also pool the withheld resources and produce elsewhere. Its payment increase must cover that joint outside value. This is the strongest incentive contract displayed; additional bargaining floors are separate constraints.

    The scalar-demand counterpart of TCWM is Moulin–Sprumont’s established Strong Group Monotonicity. The proposed contributions here concern quantitative funding and resource-report domains. A protected coalition member may restore zero resources while another member changes its report. Owner identities and eligibility remain fixed across reports. All-profile proofs cover real feasible withdrawals; finite computations are evidence or discovery tools, not substitutes for those proofs.
