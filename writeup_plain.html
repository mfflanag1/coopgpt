Research campaign · cooperative game theory · July 22–24, 2026

The Fair-Division Hunt: what we found, in plain language

Two days, three papers' worth of results, one fifty-year-old corner of economics. Here is everything the campaign produced — what each result says, why anyone cares, and how sure we are — written so you don't need the math to follow it.

The playing field, in plain words

Imagine several people who can team up to make money. Every possible team has a known earning power — maybe Alice and Bob together can make \$10, Bob and Carol \$8, all three \$15. Cooperative game theory asks: if everyone works together, how should the total be split?

Two ideas dominate the field:

  • Stability (the "core"): no sub-team should be able to storm off and do better on its own. A split with that property is called a core split. Sensible rules should pick core splits when they exist.
  • Monotonicity (no punishment for success): if one team's earning power goes up, nobody on that team should get less than before. Obvious-sounding — and famously impossible to guarantee in general: a classical theorem (Young 1985; Housman–Clark 1998) says no rule can be both core-picking and monotonic once there are four or more players.
The thread connecting everything below The whole campaign lives in the gap that theorem leaves open: the impossibility is proven for the broadest class of games. On nicer classes of games, maybe fairness and stability can coexist after all. Where exactly is the line? Nobody had mapped it.

Result 1 — The impossibility proof, sharpened to its minimum new theorem

The classical impossibility was proven with a set of five interlocking games. Textbooks later used three. We proved three is exactly the minimum — with two games you can never derive a contradiction (we give the one-paragraph reason), and we built a self-contained three-game certificate whose contradiction fits in six lines.

What it means: a 27-year-old classic now has its sharpest possible form. Modest but permanent — the kind of footnote-sized fact that gets cited whenever the theorem is taught.

Result 2 — The map of where the "repair" solution breaks four refutations

Because the classical nucleolus (the field's favorite single-point rule) fails monotonicity, two researchers — Arin and Katsev — spent a decade building a replacement, the SD-prenucleolus, and proved it behaves well on two special classes of games. Nobody knew how far that good behavior extends. Our answer: not one step further.

  • Its monotonicity fails on exact games (the very next class up) with an explicit 4-player example.
  • Its uniqueness property fails there too — spectacularly (a whole line segment of solutions where there should be one point).
  • On their own home turf (convex games), it fails a different fairness test — population monotonicity — with a tiny 3-player example, and the failure is typical, not rare.
  • On market-style games, it inherits the nucleolus's failures and even amplifies them — one little 4×4 matrix breaks both rules at once.

What it means: every advertised virtue of the decade-long repair program turns out to be a special feature of convex games, not of the solution. This became Paper 1 (16 pages, survived six rounds of expert review, submission-ready for the International Journal of Game Theory).

Result 3 — A published open question, answered open question resolved

This is the headline. In a 2021 paper in Games and Economic Behavior, Bas Dietzenbacher studied when an egalitarian ("as-equal-as-possible") division rule stays stable, and closed by writing that whether this always works on exact games "is an interesting open question for future research." Exact games are a well-behaved class — for up to four players the answer was provably yes.

We answered it: no. There is a five-player exact game where the egalitarian procedure talks itself into impossible promises. The example is simple enough to state at a dinner table: the procedure computes what each player can "fairly claim," and every one of the five players ends up with a fair claim of exactly 1 — but the whole pie is only 4.

fair claims: 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 5 whole pie: 4 Five promises, four dollars. The egalitarian split must break a promise — and when it does, a sub-team has a reason to walk. Stability fails.

Five players is the fewest possible. A padding trick extends the example to every larger number of players, so the threshold is sharp: up to four players egalitarianism and stability coexist on exact games; from five they cannot be guaranteed. We also found why the question survived five years: random testing almost never generates the thin-cored games where this happens — 99.4% of naively generated exact games sit in a "safe zone." Our generator aims directly at the frontier.

What it means: a question posed in print by an active researcher in a top field journal is now settled, with the smallest possible example, an infinite family, and the mechanism identified. This became Paper 2 (8 pages, "Exact games need not be egalitarian stable"), verified by four independently written implementations and three independently discovered examples. The verification supplement runs on a bare laptop with one command.

Result 4 — A new candidate for the "holy grail" rule candidate theorem, partly proven

Back to the big gap: does any rule manage to be both core-picking and monotonic on exact games? Every famous rule fails there — including, after Result 3, the egalitarian one. But the campaign turned up a fresh candidate: the leximin-above-floors rule ("make the poorest player's gain as large as possible, then the next poorest, and so on, inside the stable region").

  • Tested: over 2,000 adversarial experiments across two independent implementations, including the exact thin-core corner where everything else dies — zero failures.
  • Proven: the "exact games" fine print is genuinely necessary (drop it and we exhibit certified failures), and the technical bridge lemma that reduces the whole question to one clean statement.
  • Open: that final statement — a precise geometric property of leximin points — resisted two serious proof attempts and is now stated, with all evidence, as the campaign's flagged frontier.

What it means: if the last lemma falls, the fifty-year-old impossibility gets its definitive positive counterpart: a concrete, computable rule that is fair and stable on exact games. This is Paper 3 in draft-fragment form — an honest conditional theorem plus one unconditional one.

Result 5 — The second half of the question, closing in strong evidence, proof in progress

Dietzenbacher's question had a second half: does egalitarian stability hold for stable-core games (a different niceness condition)? Here the evidence points the other way — toward yes: nearly 36,000 stable-core market games all pass; the classic "big boss" family all passes; and in that family we proved the two failures are physically locked together — the same slack that breaks stability breaks egalitarianism, so you can't have one without the other. Along the way we corrected a published folklore claim (big-boss cores are not automatically stable — certified counterexamples).

What it means: the full answer to Dietzenbacher's question may end up beautifully asymmetric — no for exact games (our theorem), yes for stable-core games (in progress). The hunt for the general proof continues.

How the machine worked

The method is the one that cracked several famous conjectures in 2026: simple search inside a disciplined loop.

  • Exact arithmetic only. Every claimed number is certified in exact fractions — no floating-point claim survives on its own. (Day one, we caught a mainstream computer-algebra package returning provably wrong answers on three-variable problems, and wrote our own solver.)
  • Independent re-implementation. Every headline object was verified through at least two — usually four — separately written programs, each first forced to reproduce known textbook examples.
  • Adversarial review. Papers went through repeated expert review; the reviewer refuted one of our conjectures outright (we verified their counterexample and rebuilt the section around it) and surfaced decisive prior art twice.
  • The catch ledger. Across the campaign the discipline caught twelve false or overstated claims before they shipped — our own, our agents', and one from the published literature. Every one is logged. That ledger is why the results above can be trusted.

Mini-glossary

Core
The set of splits no sub-team can profitably walk away from. "Stable" splits.
Coalitional monotonicity
If a team's earning power rises, none of its members should end up poorer.
Exact game
A game where every team's earning power is "honest": for each team there's a stable split that pays it exactly its own worth. A well-behaved middle class of games.
Large core / thin core
Roughly, how roomy the set of stable splits is. Roomy cores are safe; the interesting failures all live where the core is a thin sliver.
Egalitarian stable
The equal-as-possible division rule happens to land on a stable split.
Leximin-above-floors
Among stable splits, first maximize the worst-off player's gain, then the next, and so on. The new candidate rule.
